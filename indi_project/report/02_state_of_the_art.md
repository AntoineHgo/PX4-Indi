# 2. State of the art

This chapter reviews the literature and open-source code on incremental nonlinear dynamic inversion (INDI) for multicopter rate control. It is written for a control engineer who knows PID rate control but not INDI. Derivations are in chapter 3 and are not repeated. Every statement is traced to a reading note through the bibliography keys. Papers were read from text extractions, not page images, so equations that the notes flag as reconstructed are avoided or flagged in the text. Topics on the reading list that were not read are named as limitations of the review and not written from memory.

## 2.1 From NDI to INDI

Nonlinear dynamic inversion (NDI) writes the plant as $\dot\omega = f(\omega) + G(\omega)u$ and chooses the input that cancels the model and imposes a desired acceleration $\nu$, leaving a single integrator with designed error dynamics [acquatella2017, Sec. 3.1]. The price is that $f$ and $G$ must be known. For a rotorcraft, $f$ contains aerodynamic moments that depend on airspeed and attitude, and any model error appears directly as acceleration error. Acquatella et al. call the law "sensitive to model uncertainties" [acquatella2017, Sec. 3.1].

INDI removes $f$ by measuring it. A first-order expansion about the state and input one sample earlier gives a command equal to the previous actuator value plus $G_0^{-1}(\nu-\dot\omega_0)$, with $\dot\omega_0$ the previous angular acceleration [acquatella2017, Eqs. 14-15]. The approximation is that $f$ does not change over one sample, assumed without a bound on the sample time [acquatella2017, Eq. 12]. Only the control effectiveness remains as model. INDI thus trades model dependence for sensor dependence: it needs an angular acceleration and an actuator state that are consistent in time. Wang et al. call it "sensor-based" and note that it still needs the effectiveness; the phrase "model-independent" does not appear in their paper [wang2019, p. 1118], and this report avoids it.

Time-delay control (TDC) estimates the unmodelled part from its value one delay earlier, and Acquatella et al. show that the TDC and INDI laws are the same expression [acquatella2017, Sec. 3.3]. Sampled at $t_s$ with first-order error dynamics it is an incremental PI with $K=(\bar g t_s)^{-1}$, where $\bar g$ is the effectiveness gain; second-order error dynamics give an incremental PID [acquatella2017, Eqs. 23-30]. The printed parallel-form constants are inconsistent: differencing Eq. 27 gives an integral increment $T_I^{-1} t_s e(k-1)$, which Eq. 28 prints without $t_s$, and matching Eq. 24 to Eq. 28 gives $T_I^{-1}=k_P/\bar g$ rather than the $T_I=k_P^{-1}$ of Eq. 29. Eq. 29 holds for the form $K\,(e+T_I^{-1}\!\int e)$, which gives an integral gain $k_P/(\bar g t_s)$ (checked against the paper's text for this report). Only the structure is used here. The consequence, drawn in the note and not in the paper, is that with a fixed $\bar g$ an INDI rate loop is a fixed-gain PI, and any advantage over PID must come from scheduling $\bar g$, from measured cancellation of unmodelled terms, or from actuator-state feedback [acquatella2017, note Sec. 7]. The paper's only evidence is one simulated pitch-rate loop under matched conditions, with no mismatch case and no separately tuned PID [acquatella2017, Sec. 4].

Early INDI work justified dropping the state-dependent term by time-scale separation [smeur2016, Sec. III.A]. Wang et al. call this "not mathematically rigorous" [wang2019, p. 1119], and Steffensen et al. say "the classic INDI derivation contains some inaccuracies" [steffensen2023, p. 6]. Smeur et al. credit the second-order filter used in practice to Bacon and Ostroff [smeur2016, Sec. I]. Sieberling et al.'s robustness analysis and angular-acceleration prediction (2010) was on the reading list but was not read for this report; its results are not used, which is a limitation of this review.

## 2.2 INDI for multirotors: the MAVLab line

The reference derivation is Smeur, Chu and de Croon [smeur2016]. Angular acceleration is written with a first effectiveness matrix $G_1$ (thrust and drag moments per squared rotor speed) and a second matrix $G_2$ for the rotor spin-up reaction [smeur2016, Eqs. 6-10]. The rotor-momentum equations are not reproduced here, because the text extraction garbles them; chapter 3 carries the model, with its equations checked against the PDF pages (`review/ch3_equation_check_s23.md`).

**Acceleration estimation and filter synchronisation.** The gyro is passed through a second-order low-pass $H(s)$, differentiated, and the same filter is applied to the actuator signal so that the increment is built from equally delayed quantities. The values are $\omega_n=50$ rad/s and $\zeta=0.55$ at a 512 Hz loop [smeur2016, Sec. III.B]. With matched filtering, "all that remains is the actuator dynamics" [smeur2016, Sec. III.C]. The flight ablation supports this: without delay compensation the vehicle oscillated severely, the doublet was not flown for safety, and the authors call the compensation "crucial" [smeur2016, Sec. VI.A, Fig. 11]. They reject predictive filtering as needing extra modelling and unable to predict disturbances [smeur2016, Sec. I]. Wang et al. add that "synchronization errors still exist in practice" [wang2019, p. 1123]. Tal and Karaman require "identical LPFs" on acceleration and thrust [tal2021, Sec. III-B], and Blaha et al. put all incremental-model signals through one 15 Hz Butterworth [blaha2024, Sec. II-A.a].

**Actuator dynamics and delay.** Smeur et al. assume the same first-order actuator on all motors, using measured rotor speed where available and a model otherwise; the time constant is not given in the text read [smeur2016, Sec. III.B-C]. Their cascaded paper gives $\alpha=0.1$ at 512 Hz for the Bebop and shows that attitude gains designed without the actuator can be unstable with slower actuators ($\alpha=0.02$) [smeur2018, Eqs. 11-12]. Steffensen et al. show that with equal actuator bandwidth $\omega$, classical INDI makes the output derivative follow the virtual control through a first-order lag of bandwidth $\omega$ [steffensen2023, Eq. 29], and that INDI approximates NDI with actuator dynamics as $\omega$ grows without reproducing its designed error dynamics [steffensen2023, Appendix]. This is a SISO fixed-wing simulation, and the paper does not say whether $u_0$ is the measured actuator state or the previous command [steffensen2023, Sec. V, Eq. 18]. That distinction matters when no rotor-speed measurement exists.

**$G_1$ and $G_2$.** $G_2$ is non-zero only in yaw. Smeur et al. report its effect as "very significant": yaw-acceleration change matched flight with $G_2$ and was far off without it, flights without it oscillated or could not take off, and flying without it needed an inflated yaw effectiveness at a "severe performance penalty" [smeur2016, Sec. VI.D]. Dropping gyroscopic coupling and treating the rotor as linear cost about 0.2 % and 0.9 % in a least-squares fit on one dataset [smeur2016, Sec. III.A]. Tal and Karaman use $(\mu;T)=G_1\varpi^{\circ2}+G_2\dot\varpi$ [tal2021, Eq. 5]. Practitioners disagree: one forum poster says $G_2$ "can be ignored first" [impl_px4_prior, Sec. 4B], and in the Paparazzi example $G_2$ is about 60 times the yaw $G_1$ [impl_paparazzi, B4]. I take the flight evidence as more reliable and treat $G_2$ as needed for yaw.

**Performance evidence.** On a 396 g Bebop, a 30 degree roll doublet reached the reference in 0.2 s with very small overshoot, and a hand-tuned PID was similar; INDI "marginally improves" responsiveness [smeur2016, Sec. VI.A]. For a persistent pitch-moment step, INDI returned to zero in about 0.3 s against about 1.5 s for PID [smeur2016, Sec. VI.B]. This is one airframe, one hand-tuned PID, one doublet per axis, no repetition count and no tabulated metrics [smeur2016, note Sec. 7]. It shows INDI rejecting a step moment faster than a PID without a tuned integral, not a comparison with a retuned PID.

## 2.3 Control effectiveness identification

*Fixed, from bench data.* Tal and Karaman use "static tests" and a regression throttle map with an integral correction for battery voltage [tal2021, Sec. III-E, V-A]. The paper does not describe the identification procedure or give the identified values. They show in simulation that the incremental loop tolerates large effectiveness error [tal2021, Eqs. 46-48].

*Offline from one flight.* Smeur et al. solve for $G_1,G_2$ by least squares from a logged flight, avoiding inertia and actuator-force knowledge [smeur2018, Sec. 2.2].

*Online adaptive.* Smeur et al. use an LMS filter on the filtered increments, with a slower estimation filter ($\omega_n=25$ rad/s) because differentiation amplifies noise [smeur2016, Sec. IV]. A coarse initial $G$ is still needed. With added bumpers, adaptation restored nominal doublet performance, but the evidence is experimental, with no convergence proof and no learning-rate values [smeur2016, Sec. VI.C; note Sec. 8]. Adaptation diverged briefly at take-off and landing [smeur2016, Sec. VI.C]. In the cascaded paper the thrust row is also adapted, and converged values from two initial values agreed with the offline value [smeur2018, Sec. 4.2, Table 1]. Blaha et al. identify 52 parameters (effectiveness matrices plus per-motor speed limit, curvature, idle speed and time constant) by recursive least squares with forgetting, from a throw, in 450 ms; 57 launches all stabilised, with logs for 20 [blaha2024, Sec. II-B, III-A]. Live fits differed from bench values by 10 to 35 %, by about a factor two for yaw effectiveness of two motors, and a fitted curvature above 1 is not discussed [blaha2024, Table I]. The authors say bench values are "not a ground truth". The method needs high-rate rotor speed and has no PID baseline [blaha2024, Sec. IV].

Two conclusions follow. Effectiveness need not be accurate, as live recovery worked with fits tens of percent from bench values, though no sensitivity study fixes a tolerance [blaha2024, note Sec. 9]. And the safeguards around adaptation matter as much as the estimator (section 2.7). Acquatella et al. note that a constant $\bar g$ loses INDI's self-scheduling and propose scheduling it online [acquatella2017, Remarks 2, 4].

## 2.4 Saturation and allocation

Saturation is the least settled topic. Smeur et al. describe no saturation, rate limit or anti-windup handling [smeur2016, note Sec. 6]. In the cascaded paper they state that saturation can destabilise the loop, that pseudo-inverse allocation then realises the objective suboptimally, and that prioritised allocation is work in progress [smeur2018, Sec. 8]. Acquatella et al. do not discuss it [acquatella2017, note Sec. 5]. Wang et al. include actuator limits only in simulation, where high gain "degrades the performance" and can diverge [wang2019, p. 1125].

*Pseudo-inverse.* Smeur et al. invert a matrix that includes $G_2$ [smeur2016, Eq. 19; smeur2018, Eq. 7]. Steffensen et al. warn that when the allocation matrix differs from the true input matrix, uncontrolled states can depend on the allocation [steffensen2023, p. 4].

*Command-space bounds and reallocation.* Tal and Karaman move the yaw moment first, then reduce thrust, then clip [tal2021, Sec. III-E]. Their drag-plate test shows the limit: the yaw moment "causes motor speed saturation and very large momentary yaw tracking error", with a maximum yaw excursion of 51.7 degrees against 5.3 without the plate [tal2021, Sec. V-D, Table V]. The yaw labels of Tables IV and V differ, so they should not be compared across tables [tal2021, note Sec. (d)].

*Actuator-space INDI.* Blaha et al. pose the incremental model in actuator space and solve by pseudo-inverse or, if bounds must hold, an active-set method. The paper does not say which ran in flight [blaha2024, Sec. II-A; note Sec. 6]. Paparazzi's weighted least-squares allocator is described in section 2.7.

No paper treats saturation inside the recursion, where the base of the increment must be the command actually applied. Paparazzi does this implicitly by clipping before the actuator model; I found no source that analyses it.

## 2.5 Outer-loop INDI, cascades and differential flatness

Smeur et al. extend INDI to linear acceleration [smeur2018]. The outer loop rotates the accelerometer measurement to the inertial frame and computes roll, pitch and thrust increments toward a desired acceleration, with aerodynamic partials set to zero as "the best guess" [smeur2018, Sec. 3, Eqs. 16-19]; a position PD supplies the desired acceleration. A nonlinear thrust-vector increment reduced the maximum vertical-acceleration error in one manoeuvre, though more research is needed [smeur2018, Sec. 4.4, 7]. In a 10 m/s open-jet flow the maximum position deviation was 0.21 m on entry and 0.20 m on exit for INDI against 1.51 m for PID, over 7 repetitions each. Outdoors in a 5.1 m/s mean wind the average maximum error was 0.24 m (12 flights analysed) against 0.85 m (12 flights) [smeur2018, Sec. 5-6]. The note lists cautions: the PID baseline shared the INDI attitude loop and was hand-tuned, outer gains were not matched ("crude" in the authors' words), INDI has no position integral while the PID does, INDI also used the 512 Hz accelerometer while position arrived at 4 Hz, and the outdoor blocks were not interleaved [smeur2018, Sec. 5; note Sec. (e)]. The gains printed in Sec. 2 ($K_\Omega = 28.0$, $K_\eta = 21.4$) and in Table 2 ($K_\Omega = 10.7$, $K_\eta = 28.0$) conflict, so they cannot both describe the flown design [smeur2018, Sec. 2, Table 2].

Tal and Karaman add differential flatness [tal2021]. Reference jerk and snap give body-rate and angular-acceleration feedforward, INDI laws track linear and angular acceleration, and a nonlinear inversion with closed-loop speed control from optical encoders reaches motor level [tal2021, Sec. II-III]. The position loop is a PD, not INDI, and the authors claim the incremental form removes the need for integral action [tal2021, Sec. III-B]. They show that, with ideal modelling, the angular-acceleration response is set by the motor dynamics, and that disturbance rejection is limited by the filter and motor bandwidth [tal2021, Sec. IV-A]. On a 609 g vehicle at 2 kHz they report 6.6 cm RMS position error at up to 12.9 m/s and 2.1 g; disabling jerk and snap feedforward raised RMS error from 9.0 to 16.8 cm on another trajectory [tal2021, Tables IV-V]. There is no hardware comparison with PID; the only non-incremental baseline is simulated and has no integral action [tal2021, note summary]. The claim that off-the-shelf rate loops cannot use snap is an argument from structure [tal2021, Sec. III-C]. Blaha et al. put an NDI position loop and an attitude loop on their INDI inner loop [blaha2024, Sec. II-A].

*Limitation of this review.* The UZH comparison of nonlinear MPC and differential-flatness-based control (sun2022) was on the reading list but was not read, so no claim from it is made here.

These designs show what a good inner loop enables, but the effectiveness, filter and actuator issues are decided in the inner loop, and their outer gains are platform-specific.

## 2.6 Robustness, stability and failure modes

**Formal results.** Wang et al. [wang2019] drop time-scale separation and treat sampled INDI as NDI with a perturbation $\delta(z;\Delta t)$ from the neglected Taylor terms. If it is bounded and the internal dynamics are input-to-state stable, the state is ultimately bounded by a class-K function of the bound; a local version needs only local exponential stability of the zero dynamics [wang2019, Thms. 1-2, pp. 1119-1120]. Disturbances enter the controlled states only through their per-sample increments, which shrink with the sampling interval, but the internal states see a term that does not shrink [wang2019, Cor. 1, p. 1121]. The NDI mismatch term depends on the whole control and not on $\Delta t$, while the INDI term depends on the increment and "can be reduced by increasing the sampling rate" [wang2019, Eq. 62, p. 1122]. The paper rejects an earlier claim that effectiveness mismatch "has no influence", because that derivation assumed $\dot x=\dot x_0$ [wang2019, pp. 1122-1123].

The limits matter here. Proofs assume ideal actuators and sensing, and the bounds contain unevaluated constants, so the paper gives no numeric sampling or delay bound [wang2019, note Sec. (c)]. Its only statement on lag is that the loop "is able to sustain sufficiently small lags" [wang2019, p. 1123]. Support is one rigid aircraft with gust loads: with effectiveness 30 % too high, INDI RMS errors were at least 3 times smaller than NDI's, and a 1000-sample Monte Carlo gave 31 NDI failures and none for INDI [wang2019, Tables 1-2]. NDI had the other model term exact, and the 31 NDI failure cases were discarded before the box plots and interquartile ranges were computed [wang2019, Sec. IV.D]. There is no multirotor and no flight test. The paper cites 100 Hz as adequate for rigid aircraft and 512 Hz for quadrotors, citations unchecked [wang2019, p. 1121].

The reading note on Acquatella et al. derives, and the paper does not state, that with true effectiveness $G$ and law gain $\bar g$ the acceleration error obeys $e_k=(1-G/\bar g)e_{k-1}$, stable for $0<G/\bar g<2$ [acquatella2017, note Sec. 7; note author's derivation]. A fixed-gain PID tuned at $\bar g$ should scale the same way. Steffensen et al. add that state-derivative feedback "could influence the stability and robustness" under uncertainty and noise [steffensen2023, p. 13].

**Failure modes in the sources.**
- *Filter and delay mismatch:* severe oscillation without delay compensation [smeur2016, Fig. 11]. A higher cutoff rejects gusts faster but passes more noise, and INDI was judged noisier than PID in steady flow [smeur2018, Sec. 4.5, 5.1].
- *Missing $G_2$:* yaw oscillation or no take-off [smeur2016, Sec. VI.D].
- *Actuator-model error:* the effect of a modelled rather than measured actuator state is not analysed in the papers read; Steffensen et al. assume a measured noise-free state and Tal and Karaman use encoders [steffensen2023, Sec. V; tal2021, Sec. III-E]. No source read for this review analyses INDI with a modelled rather than measured actuator state; chapter 9 reports what this project observed on that question (the H5 limit cycle).
- *Saturation:* section 2.4.
- *Ground contact and engagement:* adaptation diverged at take-off and landing [smeur2016, Sec. VI.C]; Paparazzi suppresses the increment on the ground (section 2.7).
- *Sensor bias:* accelerometer bias gives a steady position offset in the outer loop [smeur2018, Sec. 4.4].
- *Linearised inversion:* yaw oscillation when motor time constant and sample time differ greatly [tal2021, Sec. V-E].
- *Single-vehicle evidence:* each flight paper uses one airframe, and none compares a retuned PID under fixed metrics.

## 2.7 Open-source implementations

**Paparazzi.** The note covers `stabilization_indi_simple.c` and `stabilization_indi.c` at commit c04ffe40, both read in full [impl_paparazzi].

*Simple controller.* Effectiveness is a scalar per axis in rad/s$^2$ per raw command unit, with no matrix or allocation; the standard mixer follows [impl_paparazzi, A1]. The rate loop is a P term (default 28) producing an acceleration reference, and the attitude loop divides its gain (default 600) by it, giving a parallel PD with no integral [impl_paparazzi, A1]. Angular acceleration is the backward difference of a second-order Butterworth on the gyro, default 8 Hz (3.2 Hz in the example airframe) [impl_paparazzi, A2]. The actuator is a first-order model per axis command, set by a corner frequency in rad/s and discretised as $1-e^{-\omega_{act}/f}$. Its state goes through a Butterworth with the gyro cutoff, and that filtered state is the base of the increment [impl_paparazzi, A3]. There is no explicit delay term. For yaw, spin-up enters as a per-sample gain, $\Delta u_r=(\nu_r-\dot\omega_f+g_2\Delta u_{r,prev})/(g_1+g_2)$ [impl_paparazzi, A4]. LMS adaptation is off by default, runs only in flight, is unnormalised, clamps effectiveness from below at 0.01 and has no excitation check [impl_paparazzi, A5]. Each axis command is bounded (default $\pm4500$ of 9600) before it feeds the actuator model, which is an implicit anti-windup, but saturation after mixing is invisible [impl_paparazzi, A6]. On the ground at low thrust it outputs the proportional term only, because otherwise commands would "increment to the maximum before even getting in the air" [impl_paparazzi, A7]. The note records no non-finite checks, a fixed assumed 512 Hz period, and filters initialised to zero rather than to the current gyro rate [impl_paparazzi, A7-A8].

*Full controller.* Effectiveness is a matrix over actuators, solved as an absolute command in a disturbance-estimate form equal to the incremental law when unconstrained [impl_paparazzi, B1]. The default allocator is a weighted least-squares active-set solver limited to 10 iterations, with roll and pitch weighted far above thrust and thrust above yaw, and absolute bounds [impl_paparazzi, B6]. The clipped result feeds the actuator model. Rotor-speed feedback, when available, replaces the model, mapped linearly into command units with no latency compensation [impl_paparazzi, B3]. Adaptation is off by default, in flight only, bounded to between half and twice the initial value, with a TODO for the missing excitation check [impl_paparazzi, B5]. By reading the code, the adaptation scales its input by `ADAPTIVE_MU` but not its measured output, so the prediction is consistent only when `ADAPTIVE_MU` equals $1/$`INDI_G_SCALING` $= 10^{-3}$, the code default; the example airframes set $10^{-4}$ [impl_paparazzi, B5]. This is a static reading, not tested in simulation or flight.

**Prior PX4 work.** All evidence is repository and forum text read from local copies [impl_px4_prior].
- *PR 25216 (multicopter INDI rate control)* was closed by the stale bot, not by a maintainer decision. It added a module using ESC rotor speed (low-passed) as actuator state, in torque space with metric rotor parameters, publishing the PID torque plus an INDI term; its final state was not checked [impl_px4_prior, Sec. 1]. A maintainer found an increment on top of the rate-controller output "a bit strange", since the controller is robust because it "uses measurements to correct its commands". Maintainers asked for a separate module started in place of the rate controller, no changes to the commander or allocator, and objected to duplicated parameters and high-rate logging. No results were reported.
- *PR 25166 (fixed-wing iNDI)* is an outer-loop, acceleration-level design, stalled as a draft with no results [impl_px4_prior, Sec. 2]. *PRs 25863 and 25864* closed within minutes and the diff was unavailable, so no conclusion is drawn [impl_px4_prior, Sec. 3].
- *Forum.* A 2019 thread proposed NDI for multicopters; maintainers advised against an offboard rate loop [impl_px4_prior, Sec. 4A]. In a 2024 thread, ports of the lei-ss code to SITL are reported. One poster's vehicle took off and shook, with 8 Hz on motor speed against 20 Hz on rates and $G_2$ ignored, and wrote "If same, the vehicle can not take off" about setting the two cutoffs equal [impl_px4_prior; PX4 forum thread 36293, post of 2024-03-05]. The cause of the shaking is not established in the thread. Another ran INDI in the rate controller at 800 Hz, working in Gazebo and jittering on an F450 [impl_px4_prior, Sec. 4B]. No logs or patches were posted, and a late request asks for exactly that information.
- *lei-ss/indi* (2017) ports the Paparazzi simple controller into a 2016 PX4 attitude controller: scalar per-axis effectiveness, incremental form on the filtered previous command, first-order command model without rotor speed, thrust gating. The README claims only that "flight performance is not bad" on an F450, with no data, and it is incompatible with current PX4 [impl_px4_prior, Sec. 6].

None of this reports a measured result, so it offers design lessons only.

*Limitation of this review.* indiflight, the Betaflight-derived INDI firmware with rotor-speed telemetry, allocation and onboard identification, was cloned but its code was not read for this report; it is not described here.

## 2.8 Gaps addressed by this project

These gaps are limited to what the notes cover and may shrink as unread sources are added.

1. **No fair INDI-versus-PID benchmark.** The strongest evidence uses one hand-tuned PID and unmatched outer loops [smeur2016, Sec. VI; smeur2018, Sec. 5]. Tal and Karaman, Blaha et al. and Acquatella et al. have no retuned PID baseline. Because a fixed-gain INDI rate loop is an incremental PI(D), a credible comparison must retune the PID consistently with the same effectiveness, sample time and filters, with repeated runs and fixed metrics. This is the benchmark of chapters 7 and 8.
2. **No integrated INDI rate loop in PX4.** Prior attempts closed on process grounds, sat on top of the PID output, or targeted another vehicle class or an obsolete PX4. The maintainers' feedback is a specification: an optional, parameter-selected module replacing the standard rate controller, reusing existing allocation geometry, with no commander change. This is the architecture of chapters 4 and 5.
3. **Actuator state without rotor-speed feedback.** The papers use measured rotor speed or assume a measured state, and none analyses a modelled one (section 2.6). A first-order model with a configurable time constant (`MC_INDI_ACT_TC`) is an engineering choice whose consequences must be measured here, not quoted.
4. **Saturation and allocation in the recursion.** The incremental law has not been analysed with PX4's allocator, and the practical options (per-axis bounds, WLS priorities, yaw reallocation) are uncompared (section 2.4).
5. **Engagement and guards.** The sources agree on ground handling, bumpless engagement, adaptation limits and a matched filter chain, but the implementations leave checks to the user (section 2.7).
6. **A reproducible baseline.** The public attempts report no logs. The simulation measurements of chapters 6 to 8 are meant to supply one; they do not address hardware.
